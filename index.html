Naughty Teddy

In 1974 two Edinburgh psychologists let a toy bear wreck Piaget's most famous experiment, and children suddenly looked a lot cleverer. Try it yourself: drag the teddy onto a row and he'll spread it out.

What Piaget did

Jean Piaget argued that children in the pre-operational stage (roughly ages 2 to 7) can't yet conserve: they don't grasp that a quantity stays the same when only its appearance changes. His number conservation task went like this:

  1. The experimenter lays out two identical rows of counters, one-to-one, and asks whether the rows have the same number. The child agrees.
  2. The experimenter then deliberately spreads out (or bunches up) one of the rows while the child watches.
  3. The question is asked again. Most young children now say the longer row has more.

Piaget took this as evidence that young children are fooled by how things look and lack the mental operations needed to reverse the change in their heads.

The naughty teddy twist

James McGarrigle and Margaret Donaldson suspected the problem was the social situation, not the child's logic. When a grown-up purposely changes something and then asks the same question twice, a sensible child might reason: "They changed it on purpose and asked again, so the answer must be different now."

So they tested 80 children aged four to six in Edinburgh. In one condition the experimenter changed the row as normal. In the other, a "naughty teddy" came out of a box and messed up a row, apparently by accident. The transformation was identical; only its apparent intention changed.

The bars show the proportion of children who correctly said the rows still had the same number. Far more children conserved when the change looked like an accident, which suggests many could conserve well before age seven. Piaget, on this reading, had underestimated them because of how he asked.

But was teddy just a distraction?

Moore and Frye (1986) added a crucial check, which you can try with the "sneaky teddy" switch above. In their version teddy sometimes actually added or removed a counter. Many children still said "the same", even though the number really had changed. That suggests some children in the teddy condition weren't reasoning about number at all: they were simply distracted, or were ignoring the transformation because it seemed unimportant.

Other studies back up the social-context explanation, though. Samuel and Bryant (1984) found children did better when asked only once, after the transformation. Light, Buckingham and Robbins (1979) got similar gains by giving children an "accidental" reason to pour pasta shells into a new container, a chipped beaker. Taken together, the research suggests Piaget's tasks made children look less capable than they were, but that the naughty teddy result alone isn't clean proof of conservation.

Evaluation at a glance

Strengths include tight control (the only thing varied was who made the change) and a large sample. Weaknesses include possible distraction effects, the artificial lab setting, and the fact that a correct "same" answer doesn't show why the child said it.
